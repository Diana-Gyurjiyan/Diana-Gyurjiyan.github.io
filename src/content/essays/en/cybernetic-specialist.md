## 1. Introduction

The current developments in artificial intelligence (AI) bring with them a cruel paradox: machines
are not becoming more human, but human beings are voluntarily reducing their capacity for thought
and agency to a static process. The culprits in this process: the cybernetic specialist. The
cybernetic specialist is the contemporary embodiment of Jean-Paul Sartre’s café waiter (_garçon de
café_).

In this essay, I examine how the AI hype fuels a culture of _mauvaise foi_ (bad faith). In practice,
we see highly theoretical domain knowledge flattened into marketable slogans. Pseudo-experts
construct façades that invite society to recklessly neglect its own freedom and craftsmanship. If
everything is handed over to autonomous agents and the user is merely one message away from
triggering a series of actions, who, ultimately, is the subject? Why do we so willingly surrender
our capacity for judgment? There is another impending danger: craftsmanship disappears when juniors
skip the friction of learning.

## 2. The Perversion of the Façade

Every paradigm shift creates its own priestly class. In our era, this class consists of the
self-proclaimed AI strategist, prompt guru, and cybernetic consultant. These self-proclaimed
specialists are armed with hastily memorised jargon with which they stage a spectacle of
mastery.[^1] Beneath this performance, however, lies a fundamental misconception. Complex
mathematical formulas and the formal limitations of computational models are reduced to digestible,
popularised, and marketable buzzwords. Proponents would call this the democratisation of knowledge,
but in reality, it merely extends the theatrical set.

In _Being and Nothingness_, Jean-Paul Sartre describes the café waiter whose movements are just a
little too exaggerated, a little too emphatic, and a little too fast:

> _"Let us consider this waiter in the café. His movement is lively and forward, a little too
> precise, a little too rapid. He comes toward the customers with a step a little too quick... All
> his behavior seems to us a game... He is playing, he is amusing himself. But what is he playing?
> We need not watch long before we can explain it: he is playing at being a waiter in a café."_

According to Sartre, the waiter tries to embody his social role so completely that he appears to
deny his own freedom. His identity seems to coincide with his function: he is a waiter, and a waiter
simply does what a waiter is supposed to do. But this is precisely where the existential
misconception lies. A human being is not a waiter in the same way that a table is a table. A table
coincides with what its essence is; a human being, by contrast, can always relate to what they are,
to the roles they fulfil, and to the possibilities they have not yet realised. Sartre thereby
describes the tension between the facticity of our existence, our actual circumstances, history, and
social roles, and our transcendence: the capacity to project ourselves beyond these givens.

The waiter attempts to escape this tension by playing his role as though it determines his entire
being. His movements are just a little too precise, just a little too fast, and just a little too
emphatic. He performs his function as though there were no distance between the person he is and the
role he fulfils. In doing so, he treats himself as an object with a fixed essence, even though he
remains a free subject who must relate to that role. His bad faith does not lie in the fact that he
is a waiter or takes his work seriously, but in his attempt to conceal his freedom behind that
function. He acts as though his behaviour is entirely determined by what his role demands of him,
even though he could relate to that role differently.

Why, then, is the Cybernetic Specialist Sartre’s contemporary café waiter? Not because he happens to
carry the title of AI expert, nor because the use of new technology is itself a form of bad faith.
The problem arises when his professional identity becomes entirely synonymous with the role
prescribed to him by the market. He no longer presents himself as a free subject capable of
critically evaluating technology, but as the necessary embodiment of a technological revolution.

His view of reality consequently becomes narrower. Problems appear to him in advance as
opportunities for automation, LLMs (Large Language Models), or agentic workflows. The question of
whether these technologies are actually suitable becomes subordinate to the need to apply them. His
statements contain the right words, his presentations the right diagrams, and his LinkedIn posts the
right buzzwords. Yet behind this façade, an uncomfortable question threatens to disappear from view:
do I truly understand what I am selling, or do I derive my identity from the expectation that I
understand it?

The resemblance to Sartre’s waiter therefore lies not primarily in the performance of expertise, but
in the attempt to treat a contingent professional identity as a fixed given. The Cybernetic
Specialist behaves as though technological development leaves him no choice. If the market shifts
from RAG to agentic AI, he shifts with it; if a new paradigm is announced tomorrow, he will adapt
once again. That may be rational professional flexibility. It becomes an expression of bad faith
only when he denies his ability to step back, doubt, and consider alternatives, when he behaves as
though he has no choice but to follow along.

The perversion goes deeper still when this façade becomes a norm imposed on others. The developer
who first wants to understand the theoretical foundations, the engineer who asks whether a simpler
system would suffice, or the thinker who needs time to achieve conceptual precision is then
dismissed as slow, cynical, or anachronistic. The Cybernetic Specialist does not merely evade his
own responsibility to exercise critical judgment; he also helps create a culture in which such
judgment is regarded as a lack of progress. The façade is no longer innocent theatre. It becomes a
mechanism of exclusion that pressures others to adopt the same role and subordinate their own
capacity for judgment to the demands of the hype.

## 3. Voluntary Bankruptcy

What makes the current era so alienating is the misplaced pride that has emerged around intellectual
passivity. A cultural shift has taken place: competence is no longer measured by the depth of
someone's analysis or craftsmanship, but by the extent to which they manage to outsource their
thinking entirely to an "agentic workflow".

This is a textbook example of what Jean-Paul Sartre calls _mauvaise foi_ (bad faith): the human
being's flight from their own existential freedom. According to Sartre, we are "condemned to be
free". This means that there is no predetermined human essence; no higher purpose has been
prescribed to us, and we were certainly not born to serve merely as a radar in an AI revolution. The
human subject shapes itself through the choices it makes. Yet, at the beginning of this
technological upheaval, we are witnessing a dangerous movement. Although many organisations still
approach agentic workflows[^2] with caution, or even ban them from their operations, their
large-scale integration is ultimately inevitable. The risks are already visible: there have recently
been incidents in which an autonomous agent ignored its instructions and began taking actions on its
own authority, including a hack.[^3]

This is where the existential problem lies. The problem is not that a human being uses an agent to
perform a task, but that they can hide behind that agent as soon as the outcome is called into
question. A developer who lets an agent write code, critically assesses the results, and
investigates the relevant risks acts in a fundamentally different way from someone who puts
generated code into production without understanding or checking it, only to declare when something
goes wrong: "The agent wrote it." In the first case, execution is delegated while human judgment is
retained. In the second, delegation threatens to become a denial of one's own responsibility. The
user treats the outcome as though it had come about outside their own sphere of action, even though
they themselves decided to deploy the system and accept its output. This is the existential flight
that Sartre's concept of _mauvaise foi_ helps bring into view: not the use of an instrument, but the
attempt to conceal one's own involvement in the action.

This problem becomes more urgent as agents operate more independently and acquire greater powers.
When users can no longer reconstruct why an agent performs certain actions, there is a risk that the
distance between decision and responsibility will continue to grow. Technical complexity then
becomes a hiding place: people invoke the model, the workflow, or the opacity of the system to avoid
having to exercise judgment themselves. But technical autonomy is not a moral licence. The fact that
a system can execute actions independently does not mean that the human decisions determining its
deployment, permissions, and boundaries cease to exist.

To extend this logic into practice: what happens when things go wrong on a large scale in critical
domains? Consider banks, medical institutions, or legal organisations that decide to make themselves
dependent on agentic workflows. When an automated medical diagnostic recommendation fails or a
financial algorithm makes a devastating choice, the question arises: where has the subject gone?
Many autonomous agents now operate without a human user directly at the controls. Within this web of
automation, a vicious cycle of finger-pointing emerges: the user points to the developer -> the
developer points to the model -> the organisation points to the agent's provider. The result is the
complete evaporation of the concept of subjectivity. No one can truly be held accountable, yet
everyone feels liberated from the intellectual responsibility and mental friction of human thought.

## 4. The Mental Pandemic

The question that preoccupies me is this: why does this attitude of bad faith spread like wildfire?
The answer is as sobering as it is human: it is difficult to stand still when the entire society is
moving along with the crowd. In a market paralysed by the fear of "falling behind", a coercive dogma
prevails: _join the AI madness or become irrelevant._ The need to belong somewhere and to be able to
participate in the conversation is fundamentally human. Going against the current requires an
existential courage that most people do not possess. When a technological trend becomes
extraordinarily popular, is marketed as futuristic, attracts billions in investment, and is
discussed by everyone, a powerful social pull emerges. This creates a cybernetic feedback loop. It
is a closed system of mutual influence and fear. Fear drives adoption, adoption fuels the hype, and
the hype reinforces the fear. It resembles a mental pandemic. Everyone can see that in many cases
the emperor is wearing little more than a flimsy API wrapper, yet no one dares to say so out loud.
While institutions such as schools and academia struggle desperately to resist and protect
intellectual thought, the mainstream of society swirls in the opposite direction. Asking critical
questions or demanding theoretical foundations is quickly framed within corporate culture as
cynicism or a lack of "innovative capacity". Everyone puts on the same mindless everyday smile and
joins the performance.

At the individual level, the first and foremost force is the existential fear of no longer being
relevant. _"If I ignore the trend, someone else will overtake me."_ To ward off this fear,
professionals are mass-rebranding themselves as "prompt gurus" or "AI engineers". A painful spectrum
of bad faith emerges. Where one person builds complex mathematical models, agentic evaluations, and
robust software architecture, "AI Engineering" for another simply means typing a few sentences into
an LLM chatbot. The title becomes a polished façade designed to secure membership in the club and
artificially sustain one's own market value.

At the organisational level, an identical paralysis prevails. Companies look anxiously at their
competitors: _"They are using AI, so we have to use it too."_ The fundamental question of whether an
LLM is even the right tool for the problem is no longer asked. The result is absurd technological
overkill. Tasks that could be solved much faster, more cheaply, more predictably, and with better
results by a traditional deterministic system, such as a simple regex, an SQL query, or a
conventional script, are now forcibly transferred to an LLM. It is like using an industrial diamond
drill to hang a painting on a plasterboard wall. The result is expensive, causes damage, and is
unstable, but because the drill has "AI" written on it, everyone applauds.

When a technology is no longer chosen for its substantive suitability but for its marketing value,
business and technical judgment evaporate. A solution that contains no LLM is dismissed by society
as outdated before it has even been considered. Here, Sartre's _mauvaise foi_ returns in full force:
both the employee and the company hide behind the trend to avoid having to do the real, difficult
intellectual work. The question is no longer _"What is the best solution to this problem?"_ but
_"How can we wedge an LLM into this?"_ It is an intellectual bankruptcy packaged as innovation.

## 5. The Erosion of Craftsmanship

The most dangerous manifestation of this mental pandemic does not unfold in boardrooms, but among
the new generation of junior engineers, developers, and thinkers. When a beginner relies on
automated agents too early, the process of acquiring knowledge through friction threatens to be
abruptly cut short. Craftsmanship, whether in software architecture, philosophical analysis, or
legal precision, does not emerge from surveying a finished result, but from enduring the struggle
(_l'épreuve_). Consider the traditional path of a programmer: one learned the craft by spending
hours searching GitHub, Stack Overflow, or weighty books for the cause of an incomprehensible error
message. Writing bad code, manually tracing a bug at three in the morning, or rereading a difficult
theoretical passage built the cognitive calluses that we call _intuition_ and _domain knowledge_.
The most painful hours of learning were precisely the hours in which years of experience were built.
The more hours spent struggling through the friction, the greater the seniority.

Today, this foundation is at risk of being bypassed. Degrees and exams are passed without a book
ever needing to be opened. After all, the agent provides the answer. In software development, a new
generation is emerging: **"vibe coders"**. Juniors who type a few lines of natural language into an
LLM, watch the generated result work, and subsequently imagine themselves to be experts. By letting
the agent do the heavy lifting, the developer assumes the role of reviewer within minutes,
evaluating material whose underlying mechanics they have never built up themselves. The junior
developer has the agent generate thousands of lines of code at lightning speed, but does not
understand memory allocation, security risks, or the mathematical foundations. The aspiring thinker
or essayist generates polished arguments but lacks the conceptual muscle memory to sense when an
argument is built on quicksand.

The problem is not that this development places the junior in the role of reviewer. The problem
arises when they must judge material they have never learned to understand for themselves. Like the
waiter in Sartre's example, they move through this swamp of knowledge just a little too precisely,
just a little too quickly, and just a little too perfectly. The façade of mastery becomes visible,
while the underlying competence is absent. For the experienced craftsperson, AI is a tool that
accelerates routine tasks; their accumulated knowledge enables them to critically assess the output,
recognise errors, and reject the machine's work where necessary. For the junior, by contrast, AI
threatens to become not a tool but a replacement for the intellect they are still developing.
Critical thinking is then not trained, but outsourced directly.

The greatest danger of this development is a delayed societal time bomb. At present, society still
has experienced professionals who understand the underlying mechanics and can recognise and correct
errors in automated systems. But when organisations increasingly rely on employees who primarily
orchestrate existing systems without building sufficient technical or substantive depth themselves,
a future problem looms. The knowledge required to understand, maintain, and improve complex systems
is not automatically passed on when the people who possess it leave. If new generations are not
given sufficient opportunity to develop that knowledge themselves, society may become increasingly
dependent on systems whose workings fewer and fewer people truly understand.

If the friction of learning is definitively eliminated, not only does error disappear, but the
transmission of human knowledge also comes to a halt. We will become the custodians of a
technological civilisation whose blueprints we can no longer read. It would be a serious error of
thought to reduce this mental pandemic to a business or academic problem. Alienation does not stop
at the office door or the gates of the university; it colonises every facet of our daily lives.

We live in an era in which the news is no longer read with an open mind, but summarised in advance
by an LLM. Articles, columns, and analytical books are no longer read exclusively in their original
form, but are increasingly mediated by synthetic summaries and interpretations. When the media that
shape our view of the world are filtered through AI, a dangerous epistemological buffer emerges
between human beings and reality:

- We no longer need to weigh, grapple with, or doubt what we read ourselves.
- The sharp edges, friction, and nuance of human experience threaten to be smoothed away by the
  predictable, moderate average of a language model.

Sartre argued that human beings define themselves through their active engagement with the world
(_engagement_). Yet when we allow agents to formulate all our opinions, texts, creativity, and even
our communication, we withdraw from reality. Let me put it this way. We no longer write personal
letters or reflections ourselves; an agent articulates our emotions for us. Nor do we form our own
independent intellectual judgments on social issues. Simply asking an LLM to list the pros and cons
is enough. This is the ultimate manifestation of bad faith: we flee from the anxiety involved in
forming our own fallible opinions. We allow a synthetic device to feel, think, and speak on our
behalf.

When AI penetrates every facet of life, human beings transform from active actors into passive
spectators of their own existence. If all our information, culture, and interactions pass through a
synthetic filter, a society emerges that no longer consists of authentic subjects, but of a
collection of consumers responding to the echoes of an algorithm. Asking the question _"What is
still real?"_ is no longer an academic luxury, but an existential necessity. If we even delegate the
interpretation of the world around us to a machine, nothing remains of human freedom but an empty
shell.

## 6. The Reclamation of the Subject

Craftsmanship is disappearing, and human critical judgment is eroding at a rapid pace. Sartre's
waiters are appearing everywhere around us. We see them every day in our feeds: thousands of
LinkedIn posts, all performed with exactly the same perfect movements, exactly the same combinations
of buzzwords, exactly the same GPT structure, and exactly the same hollow perspectives.

After every new release from a tech giant, the cycle explodes all over again. It is an almost
comical spectacle of mimetic alienation: in 2022, everyone suddenly became a "prompt engineer"; a
year later, the same army called itself "RAG experts"; and today, everyone is a "specialist in
agentic workflows". If OpenAI were to announce a new concept to the world tomorrow, a new generation
of "experts" would be ready with freshly minted LinkedIn titles the day after. What is
philosophically most perverse about this is the glorification of the flight from responsibility.
Outsourcing one's own thought processes and actions to an agent is no longer experienced as a loss
or a lazy act of cutting and pasting, but as something to be proud of. People enthusiastically
encourage one another to do _less_ themselves, think _less_, and leave _more_ to automated systems.
They celebrate their own absence as innovation.

The real danger of the current AI hype, then, is not that synthetic intelligence will suddenly
develop human consciousness and subjugate us. The real danger is that human beings voluntarily
adjust their own consciousness downwards to match the uncritical, statistical nature of the machine.
This brings us to the heart of the existential critique. Authenticity does not demand a naïve
anti-technological sentiment or blind cynicism, but a restoration of existential awareness. It
requires recognising that no RAG pipeline, agentic workflow, or LLM can ever absolve human beings of
their fundamental accountability. Outsourcing choices to an algorithm is still a choice: it is the
choice to hide.

Difficult intellectual work, the friction of craftsmanship, hours spent struggling with complex
concepts, and the ability to endure intellectual doubt are not "inefficiencies" to be eliminated.
They are precisely the conditions that constitute the human subject. If we abandon our own judgment
out of fear of missing the boat, of not belonging, or simply of avoiding the pain of thought, we are
not being taken over by artificial intelligence. We have simply decided to give ourselves up.
